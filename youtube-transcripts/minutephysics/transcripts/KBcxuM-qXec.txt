We think of the moon as orbiting the earth, 
following a spiraling trajectory as the earth  itself orbits the sun. But this is 
wrong. Not only is the moon's orbit  NOT a spiral... there's an argument that the 
moon actually orbits the sun, not the earth! Sure, from the earth's perspective, the moon 
appears to orbit the earth. But from the sun's  perspective (or from an observer far above 
the solar system), the moon orbits the earth  so slowly and the earth and moon are so far 
from the sun that the moon's trajectory never  actually makes any sort of a loop, and in fact, 
it never even curves outwards towards the earth  but always inwards towards the sun!
The moon's trajectory is more like a  12-sided polygon with curved corners 
than it is a spiral or even a wiggly  line. Though the moon's trajectory is so close 
to being a circle it's kind of hard to see. Here's a simpler example of an orbit where a 
planet's moon's trajectory only ever curves  towards its sun - it looks like this 
hypothetical moon is actually orbiting  the sun and just getting nudged in and out 
a little bit by the planet as it orbits. This type of curve, where a circle rotates 
while attached to another rotating circle,  is called an "epitrochoid," though they're usually 
generated by rolling a circle around another  circle... but the curves are the same. It's a fun 
little bit of geometry: depending on how fast the  two circles rotate relative to each other, 
and how big they are relative to each other,  you get everything from spirals to wiggles to 
polygons with rounded corners. The faster a moon  orbits relative to its planet, or the larger 
the moon's orbit is relative to the planet's,  the more spirally the trajectory. The slower 
the moon moves relative to the planet,  or the smaller its orbit, the more wobbly or 
circular the trajectory. Looking at a whole  table of these trajectories, loops are in 
the upper right half here, and wobbles are  in this region. Orbits that never curve outwards 
- like the moon's - are in the remaining sliver.In our case, the sun is about 400 times farther 
away than the moon, and the moon orbits the earth  around 13.5 times for each trip around the sun 
- once every 27 days. Looks pretty much like a  circle. Here's a zoomed-in view of what our moon 
and earth's orbits actually look like, exactly to  scale (though the earth and moon are not to scale) 
- it does look a lot like the earth and moon are  orbiting the sun independently but side by side, 
just nudging each other in and out as they go. In order to start having a wobble, the moon would 
need to orbit about twice as fast as it does (but  at the same distance), or be twice as far from 
the earth (and still orbit once per month),  or the earth would need to be half as far from the 
sun (and still orbit once per year). In order to  start spiraling, the moon would need to orbit 
about 30 times faster at the same distance,  or be 30 times farther from the earth (and still 
orbit once per month). But in the real universe,  orbital speed and distance can't be adjusted 
independently: the farther out an object is,  the longer it takes to complete an orbit. 
It turns out the speed matters more,  so the moon would would have to come closer 
to earth where it could orbit faster in order  to have a wobbly orbit - one that even 
curves at all outwards away from the sun. There's a good reason the moon's orbit, while 
affected by the earth, never even curves  outwards away from the sun: due to the distances 
between the earth, moon and sun, and their masses,  it turns out the sun pulls on the moon with almost 
twice the force that the earth pulls on the moon!  Technically speaking, the moon is outside of the 
Earth's "Chebotarev radius" where the pull from  the earth and sun are equivalent. That means the 
net force on the moon is ALWAYS towards the sun,  and that's why the moon's trajectory never curves 
outwards towards the earth - there's never a net  force that would pull it that way. The force on 
the moon is always pointing inwards, and just gets  stronger or weaker over time depending on whether 
the earth is pulling together with the sun,  or pulling against the sun. According to 
net forces, the moon is primarily orbiting  the sun with perturbations from the earth.
Except, we shouldn't be using net forces,  because the earth and moon are moving in a circle 
around the sun, so we need to take into account  centrifugal effects. Those effects weaken the 
sun's relative pull enough that the earth's pull  on the moon is stronger. Technically speaking, 
we say the moon is inside the Earth's "Hill  radius," the zone where for practical purposes 
for objects orbiting along with the earth,  the earth's gravitation dominates the 
sun's. According to the hill radius,  the moon orbits the earth.
Though technically speaking,  the moon doesn't orbit the earth. The moon and 
the earth both actually orbit the center of mass  of the earth-moon system - that center of mass 
is inside the earth, so it's reasonable to say  the moon is orbiting the earth. But not by much! 
If the moon were exactly the same but just 40%  farther away, or in the same place but just 
40% more massive, then the earth-moon center  of mass would be outside the earth, and the 
earth and moon might be better described as a  double planet rather than a planet and satellite!
Though this would do basically nothing to change  the shape of the moon's trajectory around the sun.
The point is: defining whether something is a  satellite vs independently orbiting the sun 
is actually a really tricky and possibly even  futile endeavor. The moon is certainly on the 
boundary between the two, with the balance of  evidence leaning on the side of "satellite."
The best way to put it is that the earth and  moon and sun interact in a complex gravitational 
dance that defies simple classification - on the  one hand, it looks like the earth and moon orbit 
each other while together orbiting the sun,  and on the other, it looks like the earth 
and moon orbit the sun side by side,  pulling each other back and forth as they go. 
Both are true, from a certain point of view. ...Oh, and I definitely wouldn't rely too 
heavily on the shapes of trajectories to  think about orbits: trajectories can easily be 
used to mislead you! Like, you might imagine  that a point on earth's equator draws out 
loops as the earth spins along its orbit,  but because of how fast the earth is moving 
around the sun, points on the earth's surface  don't trace out loops but, like the moon, 
trace out something more like a wobbly circle.  Should we then say that each part of the earth
